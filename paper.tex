\begin{abstract}
Deep learning on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) could support earlier
detection of Alzheimer's disease, but privacy regulation prevents hospitals
from pooling the scans such models are trained on.  We present a federated,
differentially private and explainable pipeline for cognitively normal (CN),
mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and Alzheimer's disease (AD) classification,
evaluated on \CohSubjects{} subjects from \CohSites{} sites of the Alzheimer's
Disease Neuroimaging Initiative with subject-disjoint and site-disjoint
cross-validation.  Each scan is segmented by a neural voxel-based morphometry
pipeline and summarised as \CohFeatures{} atlas region volumes, giving a model
with \CohDim{} parameters.  The model reaches \CohBinAcc{} accuracy on CN
versus AD (AUROC \CohBinAUROC{}) and \CohTriAcc{} on the three-class task.
Federated over \CohClients{} clients formed from the real acquisition sites
with a proximal term (FedProx), it reaches \CohFedBin{} on CN versus AD.  Under subject-level differential
privacy it retains \CohDpBinOne{} at a privacy budget of one, while a
membership-inference attack falls to an AUROC of \CohAttackOne{}.  We show
that the utility cost of subject-level privacy is governed by the number of
perturbed parameters, and that explanation fidelity degrades far faster than
accuracy: at a budget of ten the private model is more accurate than its
non-private counterpart while the agreement of its region attributions falls
from \CohXaiNonPrivate{} to \CohXaiTen{}.
\end{abstract}

Alzheimer's disease, differential privacy, explainable artificial

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Structural MRI captures the atrophy that accompanies Alzheimer's disease,
most prominently in the medial temporal lobe~\cite{jack2010}, and machine
learning on MRI has been studied extensively as a route to earlier
diagnosis~\cite{cuingnet2011,wen2020}.  Models of this kind need data from
many hospitals, yet patient-privacy regulation makes centralising scans
difficult.  Federated learning~\cite{mcmahan2017} trains a shared model while
the scans stay at each site~\cite{kaissis2020,rieke2020}, but it does not by
itself bound what the model reveals about any patient: model updates and the
released model can leak training membership~\cite{shokri2017,yeom2018}.
Differential privacy (DP)~\cite{dwork2014} provides that bound, at a cost in
utility that is often prohibitive for deep networks on small medical
cohorts~\cite{ziller2021}.  Clinical deployment also requires that a model
explain its decisions, and it is not known how privacy affects those
explanations.

This paper measures the three costs together, on the same subjects and under
the same protocol: what federating over real hospital sites costs, what
subject-level DP costs, and what privacy does to the model's explanations.
Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a region-volume representation of structural MRI, obtained with neural
  voxel-based morphometry~\cite{fisch2024deepmriprep}, that reaches
  \CohBinAcc{} on CN versus AD and \CohTriAcc{} on CN/MCI/AD under
  subject-disjoint evaluation, and that holds under site-disjoint evaluation;
\item federated training over the cohort's real acquisition sites, compared
  with the simulated partitions commonly used as benchmarks, showing that the
  proximal correction which helps on real sites hurts on an IID partition;
\item subject-level DP-FedAvg with numerical privacy accounting, together with an
  analysis showing that its cost is set by the perturbed dimension, so that
  the region model remains usable at $\varepsilon = 1$ where a convolutional
  network does not;
\item a measurement of explanation fidelity under privacy, made possible
  because every feature is a named anatomical region, and an empirical
  membership-inference audit of the private models.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\emph{MRI-based classification.}  Benchmarks of structural-MRI
classifiers~\cite{cuingnet2011} and of convolutional networks~\cite{wen2020}
report that voxel-based features from tissue segmentation perform as well as
deep networks for CN versus AD, and that evaluations which split data below
the subject level overstate accuracy~\cite{wen2020}.  Cross-cohort studies
confirm the strength of modulated grey-matter features~\cite{bron2021}, and
the blinded CADDementia challenge places the best three-class accuracy from
structural MRI at 63.0\%~\cite{bron2015}.

\emph{Federated learning for Alzheimer's disease.}  Federated averaging and
secure aggregation have been applied to MRI-based Alzheimer's
detection~\cite{mitrovska2024}, to MCI-to-AD progression from multi-site
health records~\cite{pan2025}, and combined with rule-based reasoning under
uncertainty~\cite{basnin2025}.  Explainable federated models have been
proposed for brain-tumour MRI~\cite{mastoi2025}.  FedProx~\cite{li2020fedprox}
adds a proximal term to local training to limit client drift under
heterogeneous data.  These studies typically
simulate client heterogeneity; we federate over the sites that acquired the
data.

\emph{Differential privacy in medical imaging.}  DP-SGD~\cite{abadi2016}
bounds the influence of each training example, and user-level
DP-FedAvg~\cite{mcmahan2018} bounds the influence of each participant.
Applied to deep networks on medical images, DP has been shown to cost
accuracy~\cite{ziller2021}.  We account for privacy with the privacy random
variable (PRV) accountant~\cite{gopi2021}, which is tighter than R\'enyi
DP~\cite{mironov2017}, as implemented in Opacus~\cite{opacus}, and audit it
empirically with membership inference~\cite{shokri2017,yeom2018,carlini2022}.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Cohort}
\label{sec:cohort}

We use T1-weighted MRI from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative
(ADNI)~\cite{petersen2010}: \CohSubjects{} subjects (\CohCN{}~CN,
\CohMCI{}~MCI and \CohAD{}~AD) acquired at \CohSites{} sites.  Each subject
contributes exactly one scan, chosen deterministically by ADNI preprocessing
level and then by the earliest acquisition date, so that the subject is the
unit of evaluation.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Region Features}
\label{sec:features}

Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarises the pipeline.  Part of the cohort carries
image headers from which the anatomical orientation cannot be read, so the
orientation of each scanner series is measured instead: all 48 axis
permutations and reflections are registered to a whole-head ICBM152
template~\cite{fonov2011} and the best-scoring candidate is fitted per series
and array shape.  Brains are extracted with deepbet~\cite{fisch2023deepbet}
and registered affinely to MNI152 by mutual
information~\cite{garyfallidis2014}; a per-subject correlation with the
template excludes registration failures by a fixed threshold.

Region features are produced by deepmriprep~\cite{fisch2024deepmriprep}, a
neural implementation of the CAT12 voxel-based morphometry
pipeline~\cite{gaser2024}.  A trained network segments grey matter, white
matter and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), a nonlinear registration aligns the
brain to the CAT12 template, and the Neuromorphometrics atlas is warped back
to the subject, so that each region is measured on the subject's own anatomy.
Each region contributes the tissue that CAT12 assigns to it (grey matter for
cortical and subcortical regions, CSF for the ventricles) as a volume divided
by total intracranial volume, which removes head size.  The result is
\CohFeatures{} features per subject.

Centralised baselines are a soft-voting ensemble of L2-regularised logistic
regression, an RBF support vector machine, shrinkage linear discriminant
analysis and a random forest, and two tuned variants, a logistic regression
and an ensemble, whose regularisation is selected by nested cross-validation
inside each training fold on balanced accuracy.  Standardisation and
imputation are fitted on the training fold only, and classes are weighted
inversely to their frequency.

Every configuration is evaluated out-of-fold with five-fold cross-validation
under two protocols: \emph{subject-disjoint}, stratified by diagnosis, and
\emph{site-disjoint}, which holds out whole acquisition sites and therefore
measures generalisation to an unseen scanner.  Each configuration is repeated
over three seeds.  We report accuracy, balanced accuracy, macro F1 and macro
AUROC (one-versus-rest), together with the majority-class rate, for CN versus
AD, MCI versus AD, CN versus MCI and the three-class task.

The federated model is a multinomial logistic regression over the
standardised region features, with $(\CohFeatures{}+1)K$ parameters for $K$
classes (\CohDim{} for three classes).  The natural partition assigns one
client per ADNI acquisition site and pools sites with fewer than eight
subjects into a single client, so that no subject is discarded; a training
fold yields \CohClients{} clients (Fig.~\ref{fig:partition}).  For comparison
we also evaluate an IID partition and a Dirichlet label-skew partition with
concentration $\alpha = 0.5$, each over eight clients.  Training runs
\CohRounds{} rounds of \CohLocalEpochs{} local epochs.  Under plain federated
averaging (FedAvg)~\cite{mcmahan2017} each client's local optimum pulls the
model towards its own label mix and scanner; FedProx~\cite{li2020fedprox} adds
$\frac{\mu}{2}\lVert \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{w}^{t} \rVert_2^2$ to the local
loss, where $\mathbf{w}^{t}$ is the current global model.  We use
$\mu = \CohProxMu{}$, the best of $\{0.01, 0.1, 1, 3, 10\}$ on the real-site
partition, and report FedAvg beside it.

We protect each subject rather than each scan or each gradient
step~\cite{mcmahan2018}.  In every round, each subject is sampled with
probability $q = \CohSampleRate{}$; each sampled subject's local update is
clipped to an $\ell_2$ norm of $C = \CohClip{}$; the clipped updates are summed
across all clients; and Gaussian noise $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I_d)$ is
added once to the sum before it is divided by the number of participants.
The PRV accountant~\cite{gopi2021,opacus} numerically composes the privacy
loss distribution of the sampled Gaussian mechanism over the \CohRounds{}
rounds, and $\sigma$ is the smallest noise multiplier for which it certifies
$(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP with $\delta = \CohDelta{}$.  We evaluate
$\varepsilon \in \{1, 2, 5, 10\}$, for which $\sigma$ ranges from
\CohSigmaOne{} to \CohSigmaTen{}; the R\'enyi bound~\cite{mironov2017} would
need $\sigma = \CohSigmaOneRdp{}$ at $\varepsilon = 1$.  The private arm keeps
plain averaging: each local update comes from a single subject, and the
proximal term shrinks it relative to the fixed noise, which lowered accuracy
at $\varepsilon \leq 2$.

The noise is isotropic in the $d$ perturbed parameters, so its expected norm
is
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\,\lVert \boldsymbol{\xi} \rVert_2 \approx \sigma C \sqrt{d},
\label{eq:noise}
\end{equation}
whereas the summed update of $N$ participating subjects is bounded by $NC$
irrespective of $d$.  The worst-case noise-to-signal ratio is therefore
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\mathrm{ns}} \approx \frac{\sigma \sqrt{d}}{N},
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
which depends on the model only through $\sqrt{d}$.

\subsection{Explanation Fidelity and Membership Inference}
\label{sec:xai}

Because every feature is a named atlas region, a linear model's attribution
is a value per region: the summed magnitude of its weights over classes.  Two
models' attributions are therefore directly comparable.  We report the
Spearman correlation between the region importance of each private model and
that of the non-private reference trained on the same folds, and the share of
attribution mass in the hippocampus and amygdala, the regions most strongly
associated with Alzheimer's atrophy~\cite{jack2010}.

To audit privacy empirically we run a subject-level loss-threshold membership
inference attack~\cite{yeom2018}: training subjects are members and the
held-out fold supplies non-members, and the attack is scored by AUROC, for
which 0.5 is chance.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Centralised Classification}
\label{sec:res-central}

Table~\ref{tab:central} reports the best model for each task under both
protocols.  It reaches \CohBinAcc{} on CN versus AD (AUROC \CohBinAUROC{}),
\CohMciAdAcc{} on MCI versus AD, \CohCnMciAcc{} on CN versus MCI and
\CohTriAcc{} on the three-class task (macro F1 \CohTriF{}, macro AUROC
\CohTriAUROC{}).  Holding out whole sites changes these figures little:
\CohBinSite{} on CN versus AD and \CohTriSite{} on three classes.  Appending
age and sex to the imaging features raises CN versus AD only to \CohBinCov{}.
The ordering of the four tasks is the clinically expected one, with MCI the
hardest class to separate.

\subsection{Federated Learning Over Real Sites}
\label{sec:res-fl}

Table~\ref{tab:fl} compares the three partitions under both optimisers.
Federated over the real sites with FedProx, the model reaches \CohFedBin{} on
CN versus AD and \CohFedTri{} on three classes, the best of the three
partitions, against \CohFedIidBin{} and \CohFedDirBin{} on CN versus AD for
the IID and Dirichlet partitions.  Under FedAvg the ordering is reversed and
the real partition is the hardest: client-size imbalance and scanner
variation add a client drift that label skew alone does not produce, and the
proximal term corrects it.  On the IID partition, where there is no such
drift, the same term lowers accuracy.

\subsection{Privacy--Utility Trade-off}
\label{sec:res-dp}

Table~\ref{tab:dp} and Fig.~\ref{fig:privacy} report subject-level DP-FedAvg
over the real sites.  On CN versus AD the private model reaches
\CohDpBinTen{} at $\varepsilon = 10$ and \CohDpBinOne{} at $\varepsilon = 1$,
against \CohDpBinNone{} without privacy; on three classes it reaches
\CohDpTriTen{} at $\varepsilon = 10$ against \CohDpTriNone{}.  Every private
configuration at $\varepsilon \geq 2$ outperforms its non-private
counterpart: at this cohort size the calibrated noise acts as a regulariser.

Fig.~\ref{fig:law} evaluates~\eqref{eq:ratio} for the region model and for a
ResNet50, either fine-tuning only its classification head or the full
network.  With $d = \CohDim{}$ the region model stays below a ratio of one at
every budget, whereas the full network's noise exceeds its signal by more
than an order of magnitude even at $\varepsilon = 10$.  This, rather than the
privacy budget alone, determines whether a subject-level private federated
model is usable.

The law also holds for measured accuracy.  Extending the representation with
grey-matter, white-matter and CSF volumes and grey-matter density for every
Neuromorphometrics region, and with grey-matter volumes from the cobra,
Hammers, LPBA40 and Julich-Brain atlases distributed with
deepmriprep~\cite{fisch2024deepmriprep}, which add hippocampal subfields,
entorhinal cortex and amygdala nuclei, gives \AblExtFeatures{} features
($d = \AblExtDim{}$ for three classes).  Centralised three-class accuracy of
the tuned logistic regression rises from \AblBaseTri{} to \AblExtTri{}, and
at $\varepsilon = 10$ the private CN-versus-AD model improves from
\AblBaseBinTen{} to \AblExtBinTen{}; at $\varepsilon = 1$, however, it falls
from \AblBaseBinOne{} to \AblExtBinOne{}.  Richer anatomy pays only when the
budget leaves room for the extra parameters, which is why the compact
representation is used throughout.

\subsection{Explanation Fidelity Under Privacy}
\label{sec:res-xai}

Privacy changes what the model attends to far more than how accurate it is
(Table~\ref{tab:dp}, Fig.~\ref{fig:privacy}).  At $\varepsilon = 10$ the
private CN-versus-AD model is more accurate than the non-private one, yet the
Spearman agreement of its region importance with the non-private reference
falls from \CohXaiNonPrivate{} to \CohXaiTen{}, while the share of attribution
in the hippocampus and amygdala moves from \CohMtNonPrivate{} to \CohMtTen{}.
A model that is simultaneously more accurate and less explicable is the case
an accuracy-only privacy--utility curve cannot reveal.

\subsection{Membership Inference}
\label{sec:res-mia}

Against the non-private federated three-class model the subject-level attack
reaches an AUROC of \CohAttackNone{}; under DP-FedAvg at $\varepsilon = 1$ it
falls to \CohAttackOne{}, close to the chance level of 0.5.  The formal budget
and the empirical attack therefore move together.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three findings are likely to hold beyond this cohort.  First, the cost of
subject-level differential privacy is a property of the perturbed dimension:
because the noise norm grows as $\sqrt{d}$ while the summed update does not, a
compact representation over named regions is what makes a strong privacy
budget affordable.  Second, simulated partitions are a poor proxy for real
multi-site federation: the drift that real sites induce is what the
federated optimiser has to correct, and a correction that helps there hurts
on an IID benchmark, so a simulated benchmark can steer the choice of
algorithm the wrong way.  Third, explanation fidelity
should be reported beside accuracy whenever a model is trained privately,
because the two can move in opposite directions.

\emph{Limitations.}  The three-class task remains far from clinical
reliability, in line with the best blinded benchmark~\cite{bron2015}, because
MCI is a heterogeneous group that structural MRI alone separates poorly.  The
federation is simulated in a single process; clients exchange only clipped,
noised updates, but network effects are not modelled.  The feature
standardisation statistics are cohort aggregates outside the privacy
guarantee.  The proximal strength was selected on the folds it is reported
on, which makes the FedProx figures slightly optimistic.  All data come from
ADNI, so generalisation to other populations
and scanners requires external validation.  Nothing here is a medical device
or a diagnosis.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a federated, differentially private and explainable pipeline
for Alzheimer's disease classification from structural MRI.  Region volumes
from neural voxel-based morphometry reach \CohBinAcc{} on CN versus AD and
\CohTriAcc{} on CN/MCI/AD under subject-disjoint evaluation, federated
training with FedProx over \CohClients{} real-site clients reaches
\CohFedBin{} on CN
versus AD, and subject-level DP-FedAvg keeps \CohDpBinOne{} at $\varepsilon =
1$ while reducing a membership-inference attack to an AUROC of
\CohAttackOne{}.  The privacy cost is governed by the number of perturbed
parameters, and privacy degrades the model's explanations well before its
accuracy.  The code, folds and per-run results are publicly available, and a
public demonstration serves only the $\varepsilon = 10$ private model.
